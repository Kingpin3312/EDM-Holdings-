Capability Statement02
The case for one team

Every split in a package is an interface. Every interface is a risk you carry.

A typical interior package is let to four or five specialists. Partitions to one, ceilings to another, joinery, glazing and decoration to three more. Each is competent. Each holds a different programme, a different quality plan and a different view of where their scope ends.

The gaps between them are where projects lose time. A ceiling grid set out before the partition line is confirmed. A glazed screen ordered to a dimension that moved. Fire-stopping that belongs to whoever was there last. None of these appear on a tender comparison, and all of them land on the main contractor's desk as a coordination problem nobody priced.

We price the whole interior and install it with our own people, so the interfaces sit inside one contract rather than between five.
One programme
Sequencing between trades is an internal scheduling matter for us, not a negotiation between subcontractors with different incentives. When boarding has to wait for services, the same supervisor moves the crew somewhere useful.
One quality plan
The same inspection stages, the same tolerances and the same sign-off discipline apply from setting out to final decoration. The standard doesn't change hands halfway through the floor.
One point of responsibility
There is no interface argument to referee, because there is nobody to argue with. If something in the interior is wrong, it is ours.
One set of records
Wall references, inspection records, photographs and handover files are produced by one system rather than assembled from four suppliers at the end.
One commercial conversation
A single valuation, a single variation account and a single final account for the whole interior package.

Self-delivery costs more on paper than the cheapest combination of subcontract prices. It costs less by handover, because the money a split package loses is spent on coordination, rework and delay, and none of it is visible when the tender is compared.

Capability Statement04
How we build

The details that decide whether a package is easy or expensive.

Drywall looks like a simple trade from a distance. Most of the cost risk in it sits in five details, and they are all decided before anyone starts boarding.

Setting out
Every line is set from a survey of what was actually built, not from the architectural drawing alone. Structures move, slabs are poured out of tolerance and existing buildings are never what the record says. Half a day proving the grid at the start prevents a floor of walls being three millimetres out against joinery that was made to drawing.
Head-of-wall detail
Partitions are rigid; slabs deflect under load, thermally and over time. If the head detail does not allow for the movement the structural design predicts, the board cracks along the top or the studs take load they were never designed to carry. The deflection head is installed to the certified detail, with the right gap and the right fixing centres, and fire-stopped to the tested arrangement rather than the convenient one.
Fire-stopping and penetrations
The least visible work in the package and the most consequential. The right product, in the right gap, to the tested arrangement, at every point a service crosses a compartment line. Photographed and signed before it is covered, because the only way to verify it afterwards is to cut into the wall.
Risers and shaft walls
Where drywall and services have their argument. Most risers can only be built from one side, which changes the system, the sequence and the access. On paper there is always room, because on paper nothing has a bracket, an insulation thickness or an installation tolerance. We want riser sections coordinated at pre-construction, not discovered when the first duct arrives.
Acoustic continuity
A specified partition performs at the junctions, not in the middle of the panel. A well-built acoustic wall with an open ceiling void above the line performs about as well as a doorway. The same applies to glazed screens, where the head channel, the floor detail and the void above decide the result rather than the glass specification.

None of this is unusual knowledge. It is simply what a firm learns by being wrong on a live job, and it is priced into what an experienced contractor quotes.

Capability Statement08
How we know what we know

Four systems, built in-house, that follow one wall from tender to handover.

Most subcontractors price on one system, build on another and report on a third, and none of them talk to each other. When a main contractor asks how complete a level is, the answer comes from a walk-round and a feeling.

We built our own tools instead, because the thread between pricing, building, proving and reporting is where the commercial value sits. The reference a wall gets at tender stage is the reference it keeps through construction and into the handover file.

01 · Price it

EDM Measure

Drawing-based takeoff. The drawing is calibrated, then each wall run is traced and lands in a live, priced bill against a wall reference. No scaling by eye, no re-typing.

A wall-type library carries real build-ups — single and double layer, acoustic, shaft wall, linings — with supply and labour rates behind them, so a price is a build-up rather than a rate per square metre.

Revision overlay: load the new revision over the old, and what changed is highlighted. The variation is measured before anyone argues about whether there is one.

02 · Prove it

EDM Evidence

The wall register arrives from the takeoff with the same references. Every wall then runs through eight inspection stages — setting out, framing, services closed in, insulation, each layer of board, fire-stopping, decoration — signed, dated and initialled by the supervisor who looked at it.

The two stages that matter will not accept a pass without a photograph. No wall is boarded without a picture of the services inside it; no fire-stopping is signed off without a picture of the seal.

Failed checks stay on the record beside the passes. That is what makes a pass worth something.

Capability Statement09
How we know what we know

The same wall register, carried through to the report and the daily record.

Progress and Daybook close the loop. One turns signed inspections into the client report; the other captures what happened on the day, including the instructions that decide the final account.

03 · Report it

EDM Progress

The same wall register, with dates against it. Planned stage dates by zone form the programme; actual progress fills itself in from what the supervisors have already signed, so there is no double entry and no optimism.

Percentage complete comes from signed inspections rather than a walk-round, which is why our numbers stop needing revision.

Delay log: hold-ups are logged the day they happen — access, preceding trade, RFI — and closed out when they clear. Months later that dated log is the extension-of-time conversation.

04 · Record it

EDM Daybook

One page a day on the site manager's phone. Labour by trade, weather, deliveries, snags — and every instruction received, with who gave it, what they said, the time, and a photograph if there is something to see.

Verbal instructions are where subcontractors lose money. Logged in ten seconds on the day, an instruction becomes something the QS can turn into a priced variation instead of a disagreement about what was said in March.

The printed record states that instructions listed are notice of change, with formal confirmation to follow. That protects both parties.

What the thread means commercially

Pricing, inspection, programme and the daily record all point at the same wall reference. So a valuation, a progress figure and a variation can each be traced back to a record made at the time.

At valuation
The measure is a record, not an opinion. A re-measure becomes a comparison of two documents.
At variation
The instruction, the date and the revision that changed are on file before the work is done — when a change is cheapest for everyone.
At handover
The pack is a by-product of how the work was built. When a question arrives two years later, it is a search rather than an opening-up exercise.
Capability Statement10
The handover standard

Evidence, not promises.

The riskiest moment in any drywall package is the day a wall is boarded over. Once it is closed, nobody can see what is behind it, and years later somebody has to prove what was built. We decided that proof should be part of the product rather than an extra.

1

Every wall has a number

Assigned at tender stage in the takeoff, and kept through construction and into the handover file. The wall we priced, the wall we built and the wall we are claiming for are provably the same wall.

2

Every stage is inspected and signed

Setting out, framing, services closed in, insulation, each layer of board, fire-stopping, decoration. Checked in sequence, dated and initialled by the supervisor who looked at it — not marked complete by whoever was nearest.

3

Photographed before it disappears

No wall is boarded over without a photograph of the services inside it, and no fire-stopping is signed off without a picture of the seal. The record will not accept a pass without the photograph.

4

Handed over as a pack, not a promise

At completion — or at each zone, where the works are zoned — you receive the full record: every wall, every check, every photograph, as a single document your team can file, audit and rely on.

Why it matters to a main contractor

Fire-safety regulation increasingly expects a traceable thread of building information, and interior walls are where that thread most often breaks. A complete record means fewer opening-up exercises, faster sign-off, and an answer on file years later.

Capability Statement11
Before we price

A good drywall price is mostly good reading.

When a drawing package arrives, the number is the last thing we work out. First come the questions. Every one is free at tender stage and costs weeks if it is found in week nine on site.

1

Does the head detail survive the predicted deflection?

Cross-checked against the structural information rather than assumed from the partition schedule.

2

Do the fire strategy and the partition schedule tell the same story?

One is frequently revised without the other. The difference is a compartment line in the wrong place, found at inspection.

3

Is there physically room in the riser walls?

For the services drawn through them, with brackets, insulation and installation tolerance included.

4

Is the ceiling void deep enough for everything at once?

Grid, services, fire-stopping and access, together — not each in isolation.

5

What does the access actually allow?

Hoists, working hours, permit regimes and occupied-building restrictions decide real output rates more than any labour constant.

A tender that comes back with questions is worth more than one that comes back only with a number.

Capability Statement12
Mobilisation and delivery

What the first two weeks look like.

Most of what determines whether a package runs well happens before the first stud goes up.

Week one — set up
Method statements, risk assessments and material approvals submitted and signed off before work starts. Wall reference plan printed and posted in the site cabin so everyone — our supervisor, the main contractor's engineer, a visiting consultant — can see what is built, what is inspected and what is waiting. Inspection and test plan agreed. Benchmark panel programmed.
Week one — verify
Setting out taken from a survey of the as-built structure, not from drawings alone. Riser and ceiling void coordination walked with the services trades and the sequence agreed out loud, then written down.
Week two — benchmark
One area built and signed off by the client and the main contractor as the agreed level of finish, joint and lighting condition. Twenty minutes in week two that removes the argument that otherwise surfaces at handover when the cost of changing anything is at its highest.
Then — rhythm
Daily toolbox talks that say what has changed on this floor, who is working above, where the new penetration is and what the permit covers. Weekly report on a fixed day. Snags closed as we go while the trades are still on the floor and the access is still up.
Reporting

A weekly client report covering progress by zone against plan, anything behind, a two-week lookahead and the open delay log. The percentage in it comes from signed inspection records, so it does not need revising later.

Escalation
If a date is at risk, you hear it from us while there is still something anyone can do about it.
