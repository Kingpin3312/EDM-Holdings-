# 1. BRAND POSITIONING

## 1.1 The market problem

UAE specialist subcontractors sound identical on LinkedIn. Having looked across how this segment presents itself, the pattern is consistent and it is beatable.

**What most of them do:**

- Lead with a services list. "Drywall, ceilings, joinery, painting, MEP."
- Post site photos with no explanation and eight hashtags.
- Announce every Eid, National Day and Ramadan with a stock graphic.
- Use "we are proud to announce" and "we are pleased to share" as the opening line of every post.
- Show finished spaces with no detail about how they were built.
- Claim scale ("500+ skilled workforce") without evidence.
- Post nothing between projects, then three posts in a day.

**What almost none of them do:**

- Explain a technical decision and why it mattered commercially.
- Talk about the interface risk a main contractor actually carries.
- Show the unglamorous work — fire-stopping, head-of-wall detail, inspection records.
- Write like a person who has been on a site.
- Address procurement and commercial managers directly, in their language.

That last group is the whole opportunity. A Tier-1 procurement manager is not scrolling LinkedIn looking to be inspired. They are building a mental shortlist of firms that look like they won't cause them a problem. Everything below is written to land on that shortlist.

## 1.2 The position

> **EDM Holdings is the specialist who takes the interior interfaces off the main contractor's critical path — and proves it on paper.**

Three pillars underneath it, all evidenced in the V11 pack:

**One. Self-delivery.** Every interior trade under one contract, with directly employed, European-led and European-managed labour. Every split package creates an interface, and every interface is where programme and quality slip. EDM removes them by owning them.

**Two. Mission-critical proof.** Thirteen zones inside Khazna Data Centers' 100MW QAJ01 facility in Ajman, delivered for PMK Group. Data centre tolerances, sequential zoned handovers, certified fire detail documented at the point of closing in. If a firm can hold that standard, a Grade-A office floor is not a stretch.

**Three. Evidence as standard.** Every wall referenced, every stage inspected and signed, every fire detail photographed before it disappears behind a board, and the whole record handed over as one pack. Not an extra. Standard.

## 1.3 What EDM must never sound like

- A labour supply agency with a website.
- A firm whose main achievement is having a logo.
- A company that discovered LinkedIn last week and is making up for it.
- A generalist. EDM is a specialist. The word "specialist" earns money here.
- Anyone claiming numbers they can't produce a certificate for.

## 1.4 Tone rules for LinkedIn

Taken straight from the Brand Guidelines "How we sound" page, extended for this channel.

**Do:**

- Lead with the point. First line does the work.
- Be specific. A real zone, a real detail, a real sequence.
- UK English. Always. "Programme", not "program". "Metre", not "meter".
- Short sentences. Full stops are free.
- Say the awkward thing plainly. That is what reads as real.
- Write as one person, not a committee.

**Don't:**

- "We are thrilled." "We are excited to announce." "Delighted to share."
- Motivational quotes over sunset site photos.
- More than five hashtags. Three is usually right.
- Emoji bullet points. One emoji, occasionally, is the ceiling.
- American sales language. No "reach out", no "circle back", no "let's talk about your package".
- Superlatives you can't evidence. "Leading", "premier", "world-class", "best-in-class".
- Anything that reads like it was generated rather than written.

**A note on that last point.** The tells are: em dashes everywhere, three-item lists in every sentence, "it's not just X, it's Y", opening with a rhetorical question, and closing with a summary of what you just said. The posts in Sections 9 and 10 avoid all of it deliberately.

# 3. COMPANY PAGE BANNER — DESIGN BRIEF

## 3.1 The problem with the current file

`07-Social-Media/EDM-LinkedIn-Banner.png` is 1584 × 396 pixels. That is LinkedIn's **personal profile** cover size. It is well made and correctly on brand — logo lockup left, strapline right, emerald rule along the bottom edge.

On a **company page** LinkedIn renders the cover at 1128 × 191 and crops centrally. Uploaded as-is, the file loses roughly half its vertical height, the emerald base rule disappears entirely, and the logo and strapline sit tight against the top and bottom edges. On mobile the page name card also overlaps the lower-left corner, which is exactly where the cube sits.

Two files are needed, not one.

|---|---|

| Background | White `#FFFFFF` |
| Safe zone | Keep all content within the central 1128 × 150. Nothing in the bottom-left 300 × 100 — the page logo card sits there on desktop and the name block overlaps it on mobile. |

# 7. THE 90-DAY STRATEGY

## 7.1 What 90 days is actually for

Not followers. Ninety days should produce: a page that survives a procurement search, four leadership profiles that look like a real business, a body of content that demonstrates technical competence, and a repeatable weekly habit that outlives the plan.

**The measures that matter:**

| Measure | Baseline | 90-day target | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page followers | 0 | 500+ | Below ~250 a page looks abandoned |
| Followers who are main-contractor or consultant staff | 0 | 150+ | The only followers that matter |
| Profile views, Christopher | — | 400+/month | Leading indicator of BD |
| Inbound enquiries attributed to LinkedIn | 0 | 6+ | Tracked in EDM OS (Section 15) |
| Meetings or coffees booked | 0 | 4+ | The real conversion |
| Posts published | 0 | 50 | Consistency beats brilliance |

### POST 06 — The detail everyone gets wrong
**Company page · Technical expertise**

> Head-of-wall is where partitions fail.
>
> A partition is designed to be rigid. The slab above it is designed to move. Deflection under load, thermal movement, long-term creep. If the head detail doesn't allow for it, one of two things happens: the board cracks along the top, or the studs take load they were never designed to carry.
>
> Which is why the deflection head matters more than almost any other detail in the package, and why "we'll seal it up on site" is not an answer.
>
> The right detail is the certified one for that system, installed with the right gap and the right fixing centres, and fire-stopped to the tested arrangement rather than the convenient one.
>
> Get it right and nobody ever mentions it. That's the whole point.

**Visual:** A clean head-of-wall detail photograph, or a simple line drawing of a deflection head. If drawn, use emerald and ink only.

### POST 15 — Glass and acoustics

### POST 21 — Cat A and Cat B, plainly

# 12. PHOTOGRAPHY & VIDEO

The single biggest upgrade available to this brand. Right now the pack relies on drawn illustration because real photography doesn't exist yet — the `01-Brand-Identity/Illustration` README says as much. Illustration holds a website together. It cannot carry a LinkedIn page.

## 12.1 What to photograph

**High value, rarely photographed by competitors:**

- A framed floor before boarding — studs, services, daylight.
- Head-of-wall and deflection details, close.
- Fire-stopping and penetration seals, before they're covered.
- An inspection being carried out, record in hand.
- Setting-out lines on a clean deck.
- A benchmark panel with its sign-off card.
- Before and after from the identical position.

**Low value, endlessly photographed by competitors:**

- Finished empty offices with no context.
- Groups posing in hard hats.
- Hoardings and site signage.
- Handshakes.
- Anything with a drone unless the building is genuinely remarkable.

## 12.2 What must never be photographed

- Any client drawing, screen, badge or document.
- Security arrangements, access control, or anything identifying a client's operations. Non-negotiable on data centre work.
- A person without correct PPE. One photograph of an operative without eye protection undoes every safety post published that quarter.
- Untidy areas, trip hazards, poorly stacked material, missing edge protection. If the photograph would fail a safety audit, it fails as content.
- Any person who hasn't given permission.
- Faces of client staff.

## 12.3 Technical standards

- Phone camera is fine. Steady, level, and shot with the light behind you.
- Landscape for wide context, portrait for details. LinkedIn favours portrait and square in the feed.
- Never use flash on a plasterboard face — it flattens the surface and hides the joint quality that makes the shot worth taking. Use available light and shoot at the brightest point of the day.
- Clean the frame before shooting. Thirty seconds moving a wheelbarrow out of shot changes what the photograph says about the firm.
- Shoot wide, then shoot the detail. Two photographs, one subject.

## 12.4 Video

Three formats only.

**Format 1 — The 40-second walk.** Supervisor walks a zone, phone in portrait, talking. What's built, what's next, one thing that was awkward. No script, no editing, no music. Subtitles added afterwards — most LinkedIn video is watched with the sound off.

**Format 2 — The detail explainer, 60 seconds.** Kenny in front of an actual detail, explaining what it is and why it's built that way. This is the highest-value video EDM can make and the hardest for competitors to copy, because it requires someone who genuinely knows.

**Format 3 — The executive take, 45–90 seconds.** Damien or Christopher, in an office or on site, one argument, one take. Filmed at eye level, landscape or square, quiet room. Do not sit behind a desk with a laptop open.

**Never:** background music over site footage, drone showreels with a countdown, or anything that has been edited to look like a car advert.

## 12.5 Commissioning a professional shoot

Worth doing once, on the best available project, once consent is in place. Half a day, an architectural or industrial photographer rather than an events photographer, and a shot list built from Section 12.1. The output feeds the website, the capability statement, the deck and roughly six months of LinkedIn. The brief already exists in the pack as `Photography-Case-Study-Brief`.

## 13.3 From LinkedIn to conversation

LinkedIn opens the door. The `EDM-Outreach-Kit.txt` sequence closes it. The handover point matters:

1. **They engage** — a comment, a reaction, a profile view, a follow.
2. **Christopher connects** with a note referencing what they engaged with. One sentence. No pitch.
3. **They accept.** Nothing is sent for at least a week. A pitch on acceptance is the fastest way to be ignored permanently.
4. **A relevant post lands** in their feed over the following weeks. This is the actual work.
5. **A real trigger appears** — a project announcement, a job change, a tender. Now the first-touch email from the outreach kit goes, from Christopher's own address, with the capability statement attached.
6. **The ask is a coffee**, not a tender. Face to face is where this business is won.

Never move from step 2 to step 6 in the same week.

## 13.4 What to do in comments

Comments are underrated and mostly free. A thoughtful comment on a main contractor's project post is seen by their whole network, including people who will never see an EDM post.

**Good:** a specific technical observation. A relevant question. A genuine congratulation with one detail that shows you read it.

**Bad:** "Great work!" Emoji-only. Anything that mentions EDM. Anything that could be read as touting for the package.

Fifteen minutes a day, five or six comments, on posts from main contractors, consultants and developers in the UAE. Over 90 days that will do more than the posts.

# IF I WERE RUNNING EDM HOLDINGS

Five things, in order of how much they'd change the business over twelve months. This is beyond the LinkedIn brief, which is what you asked for.

## 1. Sell the evidence system, not the trade

EDM Measure, EDM Evidence, EDM Progress and the published Handover Standard are, taken together, something most specialist subcontractors in this region cannot produce and cannot easily copy. They're currently framed as internal tooling in the pack. They're a commercial weapon.

The pitch is not "we build good walls". Every competitor says that. The pitch is: *we'll hand you a wall-by-wall record of your interior, photographed before closing, signed at each stage, delivered per zone on the day.* For a main contractor carrying a golden-thread obligation and a fire consultant who wants evidence, that is worth paying more for — and it's the only argument I've seen in your pack that would let you win a package without being the cheapest.

Put it in the tender interview. Demonstrate it live on a laptop. Nobody does that.

## 2. Get one named data centre reference nailed down properly

The Ajman package is worth more than everything else in your marketing combined, because mission-critical work is the hardest credential to fake and the easiest to sell downwards. A firm that has done data centre interiors can price commercial, healthcare and government work with a straight face.

So: get the written consent, get a proper photography half-day, get a one-page reference from PMK Group, and get a named case study into the capability statement. Then use it for three years. That's the highest-return week of work available to you right now.

Four markets reads as ambition in a brochure. In a supply-chain audit it reads as something to be examined, and the capability statement already concedes the delivered record spans three jurisdictions with live delivery in one.
